🔬 The Human Auditory Spectrum & Equal Loudness

The human auditory system is capable of detecting sound vibrations ranging from approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. However, human ears do not perceive all frequencies with equal sensitivity.

According to the Fletcher-Munson Equal-Loudness Contours, human ears are exponentially more sensitive to midrange frequencies between 2,000 Hz and 4,000 Hz (the exact acoustic frequency band of human speech and baby cries). By contrast, deep sub-bass frequencies below 50 Hz require significantly more physical acoustic energy (higher decibels) for the human eardrum and inner cochlea to perceive.

At very low frequencies (10Hz–30Hz), you often feel the sound as physical skull and chest vibration rather than hearing a distinct tone. High-end audiophile headphones (like planar magnetic Sennheiser, Audeze, or Beyerdynamic models) reproduce flat bass extension all the way down to 10Hz with near-zero harmonic distortion.

🛠️ 4 Common Headphone Audio Defects Detected by Frequency Sweeps

1. The "Hair-on-Diaphragm" Buzz

If you hear a harsh, electric buzzing only between 20Hz and 60Hz, 90% of the time it is caused by a single stray strand of human or pet hair that has pierced the front acoustic mesh and is resting against the paper or mylar driver cone.

2. Driver Imbalance (Unequal Volume)

Budget earbuds often have driver tolerances with a 3dB to 5dB mismatch between left and right channels. During a center mono sweep, the phantom image will wander from side to side if one driver has higher electrical impedance.

3. Voice Coil Rub (Blown Driver)

Dropping headphones or listening at extreme volumes can cause the delicate voice coil wire to warp out of the magnetic air gap. When it rubs against the permanent magnet, severe metallic distortion occurs on low bass notes.

4. Sub-Bass Roll-Off (Poor Seal)

If bass completely disappears below 50Hz on in-ear earbuds, your silicone ear tips do not have an airtight acoustic seal in your ear canal. Switch to a larger ear tip size to immediately restore deep 20Hz sub-bass response.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can high-frequency tones damage my headphones or hearing?

Never test at maximum volume. High frequencies carry significant electrical energy even if you cannot hear them. Keep your operating system volume around 40%–50% to prevent headphone tweeter thermal overload and avoid acoustic trauma.

Why can young children hear high-pitched sounds that adults cannot?

Microscopic stereocilia hair cells located near the base of the inner ear cochlea respond to high frequencies. Because every sound you ever hear passes through the base first, these cells sustain cumulative mechanical wear and aging over decades, gradually lowering the ceiling of audible sound.

What is the difference between pink noise and white noise?

White noise has equal energy per cycle, meaning higher octaves contain much more power (sounding like intense television static). Pink noise has equal energy per octave, rolling off at 3dB per octave, which mimics the natural frequency distribution of human music and nature (rain or waterfalls).
